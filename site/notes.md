## Septic system (owner, Oct 11 2026)

- **Conventional gravity system:** a 1,200-gallon concrete tank and a standard drainfield
  (buried trenches). No pump, panel or spray heads.
- **Tank:** typically ~5 × 10 ft with two concrete lids (inlet and outlet) ~6–8 ft apart,
  often buried 6–24 in. It's usually 5–20 ft out from where the main drain leaves the
  house, which is the sewer cleanout (worksheet 13c).
- **Drainfield:** trenches leave the tank's outlet end and run downhill (gravity).
  Inspection ports are white capped pipes at trench ends. Look for green stripes in dry
  weather.
- **Rules of thumb (confirm with the county):**
  - Nothing built on the tank or the field.
  - No driving or parking on the field.
  - Keep ~5 ft between structures and the tank or field.
  - Keep the lids reachable, and the pump truck within hose reach of them.

### Making B a good sound barrier

- **Solid highway side.** No doors, windows or vents on the north (US-290) wall. Put the
  roll-up doors on the south or east side, away from the highway.
- **Height helps.** The building only shields what it hides from the traffic. A taller
  eave (~12–14 ft) covers more of the yard and the house's lower floor; second-story
  windows will partly see over it.
- **Mass and insulation.** Bare sheet metal is light and rings. Insulated wall panels, or
  insulation plus an interior liner, cut both sound through the wall and rain noise inside.
- **CMU inner wall: priced, then dropped (Oct 11 2026).**
  - **Cost:** a 40 × 10 ft wall is ~16 tons. DIY materials run **~$3,500–6,000**: fully
    grouted ~$4,000–5,900, rebar cells grouted with sand in the other cores ~$3,500–5,300.
    That includes the extra footing; see `tools/cmu_cost.py`.
  - **Why it was dropped:** the owner doesn't need quiet inside the shed, and the wall adds
    little for the house. Noise reaching the house mostly bends over the roof and around
    the ends. The path through the building (insulated north wall, the air inside, the
    south wall or doors) is already much weaker than that. The money does more as extra
    height and length, and as better north-facing windows on the house.
- The original CMU notes, kept for reference:
  - **It works on the rumble.** A fully grouted 8 in block wall weighs ~80–90 lb/sq ft,
    against 1–2 for sheet metal. Mass is what stops truck rumble.
  - **It needs its own footing.** A wall ~40 ft long and 12–14 ft tall weighs roughly 20+
    tons. Tell the building supplier and the slab engineer early, so the north edge gets a
    real footing or grade beam.
  - **Keep it separate from the metal skin.** Leave an air gap with insulation in it: two
    heavy layers separated by air beat either one alone. Let the engineer decide how it's
    braced (its own pilasters, or flexible ties to the frame).
  - **Seal everything.** Base, top and every joint; small gaps leak a lot. Carrying the
    block a few feet around the east and west corners cuts sound sneaking around the ends.
  - **Watch for condensation.** Block inside a metal building can sweat, so plan the vapor
    control.
  - **Where it helps most.** The CMU mostly quiets the inside of the building. For the yard
    and the house, sound mostly bends over the roof and around the ends, so height and
    length count most there.
- **No gaps.** Sound leaks around the ends and under the walls. Keep the slab edge tight to
  grade. The picket fence won't seal anything at the ends.
- **Longer is better.** It's 43 ft now, filling the whole gap between the oak's drip line
  (east) and the septic pipe's 10 ft keep-out (west). If probing shows the pipe is only an
  inspection port and nothing runs toward B, the building may stretch a few feet west.
- **Expectation (see Traffic noise below):** ~7 dB quieter in the yard right behind the
  building, but only ~3.5 dB at the house's west end and ~1 dB or less elsewhere. A 43 ft
  building hides a short stretch of a highway that runs past both of its ends.

### Stretching B east toward the oak (Oct 11 2026)

**Superseded by the 23 × 43 (above):** it moved west instead, so its east end is at the drip
line (25.0 ft from the trunk). Stretching east from there also runs the NE corner inside the
5 ft setback. The table and conditions below still apply if the east end ever moves.

The owner asked about adding 3–5 ft to the east end (+60 or +100 sq ft). The table shows how
much of the twin oak's root zone each length cuts off: roots heading under or past the slab,
whose edge trench severs them. The root zone used is the arborist standard of 1 ft radius
per inch of trunk diameter, so 24 ft for the near trunk. If the second trunk is similar, the
pair acts like a ~34 in tree with a 34 ft zone.

| East end | Slab edge to near trunk | Near trunk's 24 ft zone | Drip-line circle | Twin-trunk 34 ft zone |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 40 ft (now) | 25.5 ft | 0% | 0% | ~5% |
| **43 ft (+3)** | 22.5 ft | ~1% | ~2% | ~7% |
| 45 ft (+5) | 20.5 ft | ~3% | ~4% | ~9% |

- **Either is small for a healthy oak.** Common benchmarks are to keep at least half the
  root zone untouched (Austin's rule; we're outside the city, so it's only a guide), to
  dig nothing inside half the zone's radius (12–17 ft here), and to keep root cuts beyond
  3–5× the trunk diameter (6–10 ft) for stability. Both options clear all three easily.
  The second trunk is 25 ft or more from the slab either way.
- **The real risk is how it's built, not the extra 3–5 ft.** Oaks on building sites usually
  decline from:
  - compaction from trucks and stored material under the canopy;
  - fill or a raised grade over the roots;
  - an electric trench to the building cut through the root zone;
  - concrete washout;
  - torn roots from an excavator.
- **Conditions if we go east:**
  - an arborist cleanly cuts the roots along the east edge before any excavation (air-spade
    or a sharp trench cut);
  - fence the root zone during construction;
  - no grade change under the canopy;
  - bring power in from the west or the house side;
  - put the east end on piers with a grade beam instead of a deep trenched edge if the
    engineer agrees;
  - mulch and water the tree through the next summer.
- **Canopy:** the drip line sits at today's east wall, so the extra length is under the
  canopy edge. Expect to raise or shorten a few low limbs for the roof and erection
  (shot 17a still unmeasured). Cut only July–January and paint the wounds right away (oak
  wilt). Plan on leaves in the gutters.
- **More room without the oak:** deepening to 24 ft (if the trees at the SW corner allow,
  T11) adds 160 sq ft away from the oak. If probing shows the tank isn't at the septic pipe,
  the west end can also move 1–2 ft. (The 23 × 43 used both: 23 ft deep, and the west end
  moved 2.5 ft to the pipe's 10 ft keep-out.)

## Traffic noise (Oct 11 2026)

The house sits ~15–20 ft above US-290 (owner). The US-290 fence is **plain wood pickets with

it gives the ground floor ~5 dB and the 2nd floor ~1 dB.

### What TxDOT found (Oak Hill Parkway noise study)

- TxDOT's traffic-noise reports for the Oak Hill Parkway (draft Oct 2017, final July 2018)
  model this block. Receiver **R234**, the first lot west of Candelaria on US-290, appears to
  be this house (receiver map 3–4 of 16).
- **R234: 59 dB(A) in 2013, 64 dB(A) predicted for 2040** (peak-hour Leq). A home counts as
  impacted at 66 dB(A) or a 10 dB increase. This house wasn't impacted, so no wall was studied
  for it.
- **R238, the house across Candelaria by the frontage road, was impacted** (59 → 66).
  TxDOT found that a wall along the right-of-way line **up to 20 ft tall could not reach the
  minimum 5 dB reduction** (plus the 7 dB design goal) there. That is probably the
  "turned down" barrier. The other test that sinks walls is cost: no more than $25,000 for
  each home that gains 5 dB.
- The walls TxDOT did build are elsewhere: Ridgeview (south side of US-290, South View Rd to
  Scenic Brook Dr), Bell Quarry Hill Apartments, and a mobile home park at the east end. The
  2019 policy update and the 2021 validation study kept the same conclusions.

  A 12 ft wall is a structure: it needs an engineered footing and likely a permit or variance.
- **Material only needs to be solid and gap-free** (~4 lb/sq ft: tight board-on-board,
  concrete, brick). Brick is no better than any other solid wall of the same height.
- **These replace the earlier 10–15 dB figures**, which were for the straight-out path only,
  as if the wall were endless.
- **Curved or angled tops** gain ~1–3 dB over a plain wall of the same height. A vertical face
  reflects sound back across the highway, not upward. Leaning the face back sends the
  reflection up, which mostly helps the neighbors across US-290.
- **At the house**, the north-facing windows and doors are often the cheapest big gain:
  laminated or dual-pane glass with different pane thicknesses, and sealed frames.

## Plan: make the house feel less exposed (Oct 11 2026)

The house feels exposed to US-290 and to the retail and apartments going up behind it. The
goal is to cut noise, block views and protect the house's value. No single part solves it;
the shed is one part.

| Part | What it does | Limits |
|---|---|---|
| **Windows and doors** (north and west walls first) | The biggest noise cut indoors | One leaky window or door spoils a room |
| **Building B** | Yard shield, ~7 dB right behind it; blocks the view from the old carport area | Only ~1–3.5 dB at the house |
| **Wall along US-290** | Hides the road; ~4–6 dB, ~7–8 dB at 12 ft with a return along Candelaria | Ends leak; see the constraints below |
| **Back-line screen** | Hides the retail and apartments from the yard at ground level | Upper floors need tree height |
| **Evergreens, water feature** | Visual screen, and sound masking near the patio | Little real dB |

A rough rule from appraisal studies is that highway noise costs ~0.5% of home value per dB.
People also tend to find traffic they can't see less annoying.

### Hiding the road (sight lines, solid wall at the line)

The road is 15–20 ft below the lot, so hiding it takes less wall than quieting it. This
assumes the ground is about level from the house to the line and the lanes are 60–100 ft
out. If the yard falls toward the highway, add the drop.

| Viewer | Wall that hides cars, all lanes | Wall that hides trucks, all lanes |
|---|---|---|
| Ground floor, or standing in the yard | ~2–3 ft | ~4–5 ft |
| 2nd floor | up to ~8 ft | up to ~11 ft |

An 8 ft solid wall makes the road disappear from the ground floor and yard and hides most
cars from the 2nd floor. 10–12 ft hides nearly everything. The open end at Candelaria stays
visible unless the wall returns along it.

### Back line (retail and apartments)

from apartment windows 20–40 ft past the line. The lot right behind us looks like the
one-story retail (see "The development behind the back line"), so this is the worst case
until the apartments' site plan shows how far off and how tall they are.

|---|---|
| 2nd floor (eye ~15 ft) | ~11–14 ft |
| 3rd floor (eye ~25 ft) | ~17–22 ft |
| 4th floor (eye ~35 ft) | ~23–31 ft |

#### Back-line trees (Oct 11 2026)

The site is thin, rocky, alkaline soil over limestone. With the land next door cleared, the
line now takes full west sun. The area has heavy deer and oak wilt.

| Layer | Plant | Mature size | Why | Watch |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Tall, fast (back row) | **Arizona cypress** | 30–40 × 15–20 ft | Fastest tall evergreen for limestone; deer rarely eat it | Needs drainage; some canker; flammable |
| Tall, fast (alternate) | **Eastern red cedar** | 25–40 × 8–15 ft | Native, tough, takes shearing | Winter pollen; flammable |
| Middle (front row) | **Yaupon holly**, tree form | 15–25 ft | Native evergreen, sun or shade, alkaline OK | Deer browse young plants: cage them |
| Middle, shade | **Texas mountain laurel** | 10–15 ft | Evergreen, takes the oaks' shade | Slow; poisonous seeds |
| Bottom | **Evergreen sumac**, cenizo | 6–12 ft | Fills the gaps at eye level | Shrubs only |
| Long term | **Escarpment live oak** | 30–40 ft+ | The permanent canopy | Slow; prune only July–January |

- **Skip:**
  - Leyland cypress (heat, drought, disease);
  - Italian cypress (mites, too thin to screen);
  - ligustrum and photinia (invasive or diseased);
  - red oaks (they spread oak wilt);
  - running bamboo;
  - cherry laurel and magnolia (they yellow in alkaline soil).
- **Layout:**
  - Stay out of the back-line utility easement: plant 10–12 ft in from the line.
  - Stagger two rows ~8–10 ft apart: cypress ~12–15 ft on center behind, yaupon and laurel
    offset in front. The ~155 ft line takes ~10–12 big trees plus ~12–15 smaller ones.
  - Fill gaps in the existing tree line rather than planting a uniform row.
  - Put the biggest trees where the apartments' windows and balconies line up with the
    patio and the back windows (the site plan shows which way they face).
- **Planting:**
  - Plant October to February.
  - 30-gal Arizona cypress (6–8 ft) should reach ~20 ft in roughly 5–8 years with water.
  - Run drip irrigation for the first 2–3 summers, and cage the broadleaf trees against deer.
  - Under the existing oaks, hand-dig small holes with no trenching or fill, and use the
    shade-tolerant picks.
- **Keep junipers and cypress at the back line,** well away from the house (fire).

### Windows and doors

- Traffic noise is heavy in low frequencies. Compare windows by **OITC** (made for traffic
  noise), not just STC.
- Rough ranges: a standard builder dual-pane is ~OITC 20–24 (STC 26–28). Laminated glass with
  unequal panes and a wider gap is ~OITC 28–31 (STC 32–36). An interior acoustic insert a few
  inches inside the existing window can reach ~STC 40+, often for less than a replacement.
- Seal the frames, use solid-core exterior doors with full seals, and check north-side attic
  and wall vents.
