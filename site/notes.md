## Septic system (owner, Oct 11 2026)

- **Conventional gravity system:** a 1,200-gallon concrete tank and a standard drainfield
  (buried trenches). No pump, panel or spray heads.
- **Tank:** typically ~5 × 10 ft with two concrete lids (inlet and outlet) ~6–8 ft apart,
  often buried 6–24 in. It's usually 5–20 ft out from where the main drain leaves the
  house, which is the sewer cleanout (worksheet 13c).
- **Drainfield:** trenches leave the tank's outlet end and run downhill (gravity).
  Inspection ports are white capped pipes at trench ends. Look for green stripes in dry
  weather.
- **Rules of thumb (confirm with the county):**
  - Nothing built on the tank or the field.
  - No driving or parking on the field.
  - Keep ~5 ft between structures and the tank or field.
  - Keep the lids reachable, and the pump truck within hose reach of them.

### Making B a good sound barrier

- **Solid highway side.** No doors, windows or vents on the north (US-290) wall. Put the
  roll-up doors on the south or east side, away from the highway.
- **Height helps.** The building only shields what it hides from the traffic. A taller
  eave (~12–14 ft) covers more of the yard and the house's lower floor; second-story
  windows will partly see over it.
- **Mass and insulation.** Bare sheet metal is light and rings. Insulated wall panels, or
  insulation plus an interior liner, cut both sound through the wall and rain noise inside.
- **CMU inner wall: priced, then dropped (Oct 11 2026).**
  - **Cost:** a 40 × 10 ft wall is ~16 tons. DIY materials run **~$3,500–6,000**: fully
    grouted ~$4,000–5,900, rebar cells grouted with sand in the other cores ~$3,500–5,300.
    That includes the extra footing; see `tools/cmu_cost.py`.
  - **Why it was dropped:** the owner doesn't need quiet inside the shed, and the wall adds
    little for the house. Noise reaching the house mostly bends over the roof and around
    the ends. The path through the building (insulated north wall, the air inside, the
    south wall or doors) is already much weaker than that. The money does more as extra
    height and length, and as better north-facing windows on the house.
- The original CMU notes, kept for reference:
  - **It works on the rumble.** A fully grouted 8 in block wall weighs ~80–90 lb/sq ft,
    against 1–2 for sheet metal. Mass is what stops truck rumble.
  - **It needs its own footing.** A wall ~40 ft long and 12–14 ft tall weighs roughly 20+
    tons. Tell the building supplier and the slab engineer early, so the north edge gets a
    real footing or grade beam.
  - **Keep it separate from the metal skin.** Leave an air gap with insulation in it: two
    heavy layers separated by air beat either one alone. Let the engineer decide how it's
    braced (its own pilasters, or flexible ties to the frame).
  - **Seal everything.** Base, top and every joint; small gaps leak a lot. Carrying the
    block a few feet around the east and west corners cuts sound sneaking around the ends.
  - **Watch for condensation.** Block inside a metal building can sweat, so plan the vapor
    control.
  - **Where it helps most.** The CMU mostly quiets the inside of the building. For the yard
    and the house, sound mostly bends over the roof and around the ends, so height and
    length count most there.
- **No gaps.** Sound leaks around the ends and under the walls. Keep the slab edge tight to
  grade. The picket fence won't seal anything at the ends.
- **Longer is better.** It's 43 ft now, filling the whole gap between the oak's drip line
  (east) and the septic pipe's 10 ft keep-out (west). If probing shows the pipe is only an
  inspection port and nothing runs toward B, the building may stretch a few feet west.
- **Expectation (see Traffic noise below):** ~7 dB quieter in the yard right behind the
  building, but only ~3.5 dB at the house's west end and ~1 dB or less elsewhere. A 43 ft
  building hides a short stretch of a highway that runs past both of its ends.

### Stretching B east toward the oak (Oct 11 2026)

**Superseded by the 23 × 43 (above):** it moved west instead, so its east end is at the drip
line (25.0 ft from the trunk). Stretching east from there also runs the NE corner inside the
5 ft setback. The table and conditions below still apply if the east end ever moves.

The owner asked about adding 3–5 ft to the east end (+60 or +100 sq ft). The table shows how
much of the twin oak's root zone each length cuts off: roots heading under or past the slab,
whose edge trench severs them. The root zone used is the arborist standard of 1 ft radius
per inch of trunk diameter, so 24 ft for the near trunk. If the second trunk is similar, the
pair acts like a ~34 in tree with a 34 ft zone.

| East end | Slab edge to near trunk | Near trunk's 24 ft zone | Drip-line circle | Twin-trunk 34 ft zone |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 40 ft (now) | 25.5 ft | 0% | 0% | ~5% |
| **43 ft (+3)** | 22.5 ft | ~1% | ~2% | ~7% |
| 45 ft (+5) | 20.5 ft | ~3% | ~4% | ~9% |

- **Either is small for a healthy oak.** Common benchmarks are to keep at least half the
  root zone untouched (Austin's rule; we're outside the city, so it's only a guide), to
  dig nothing inside half the zone's radius (12–17 ft here), and to keep root cuts beyond
  3–5× the trunk diameter (6–10 ft) for stability. Both options clear all three easily.
  The second trunk is 25 ft or more from the slab either way.
- **The real risk is how it's built, not the extra 3–5 ft.** Oaks on building sites usually
  decline from:
  - compaction from trucks and stored material under the canopy;
  - fill or a raised grade over the roots;
  - an electric trench to the building cut through the root zone;
  - concrete washout;
  - torn roots from an excavator.
- **Conditions if we go east:**
  - an arborist cleanly cuts the roots along the east edge before any excavation (air-spade
    or a sharp trench cut);
  - fence the root zone during construction;
  - no grade change under the canopy;
  - bring power in from the west or the house side;
  - put the east end on piers with a grade beam instead of a deep trenched edge if the
    engineer agrees;
  - mulch and water the tree through the next summer.
- **Canopy:** the drip line sits at today's east wall, so the extra length is under the
  canopy edge. Expect to raise or shorten a few low limbs for the roof and erection
  (shot 17a still unmeasured). Cut only July–January and paint the wounds right away (oak
  wilt). Plan on leaves in the gutters.
- **More room without the oak:** deepening to 24 ft (if the trees at the SW corner allow,
  T11) adds 160 sq ft away from the oak. If probing shows the tank isn't at the septic pipe,
  the west end can also move 1–2 ft. (The 23 × 43 used both: 23 ft deep, and the west end
  moved 2.5 ft to the pipe's 10 ft keep-out.)

- **Gaps:** light shows between some boards, and gaps leak sound. Closing them is the cheap
  next step, and gets most of the 8 ft wall's benefit. Options are battens over the joints,
  a second layer of boards on our side, or mass-loaded vinyl.
- **Remaining gains:** a taller wall, the Candelaria return and building B add less on top
  of a sealed 8 ft fence than the table shows. Re-run `noise_model.py` with the fence as the
  baseline before spending on a wall.
- **Ownership:** the fence sits partly beyond the surveyed line. Find out who owns it (us,
  the previous owner's work on TxDOT land, or TxDOT) before modifying it.

- **The ends limit everything.** A barrier only quiets the stretch of highway it hides.
  Endless walls would give ~5–6 dB (8 ft) and ~8–10 dB (12–14 ft). The lot-length walls give
  less because traffic past the ends still reaches the house, most of all through the
  Candelaria opening. A wall gap there is likely part of why TxDOT's wall failed at R238.
- **Building B is a yard shield, not a house shield.** It gives ~7 dB right behind it, ~3.5 dB
  at the house's west end, and ~1 dB or less elsewhere. A taller eave barely changes that.
- **Height plus closing the east end matter most.** A 12 ft wall that returns along Candelaria
  is the strongest option here, at ~7–8 dB. It needs a solid gate where the drive crosses it.
  A 12 ft wall is a structure: it needs an engineered footing and likely a permit or variance.
- **Material only needs to be solid and gap-free** (~4 lb/sq ft: tight board-on-board,
  concrete, brick). Brick is no better than any other solid wall of the same height.
- **These replace the earlier 10–15 dB figures**, which were for the straight-out path only,
  as if the wall were endless.
- **Curved or angled tops** gain ~1–3 dB over a plain wall of the same height. A vertical face
  reflects sound back across the highway, not upward. Leaning the face back sends the
  reflection up, which mostly helps the neighbors across US-290.
- **At the house**, the north-facing windows and doors are often the cheapest big gain:
  laminated or dual-pane glass with different pane thicknesses, and sealed frames.

## Plan: make the house feel less exposed (Oct 11 2026)

The house feels exposed to US-290 and to the retail and apartments going up behind it. The
goal is to cut noise, block views and protect the house's value. No single part solves it;
the shed is one part.

| Part | What it does | Limits |
|---|---|---|
| **Windows and doors** (north and west walls first) | The biggest noise cut indoors | One leaky window or door spoils a room |
| **Building B** | Yard shield, ~7 dB right behind it; blocks the view from the old carport area | Only ~1–3.5 dB at the house |
| **Wall along US-290** | Hides the road; ~4–6 dB, ~7–8 dB at 12 ft with a return along Candelaria | Ends leak; see the constraints below |
| **Back-line screen** | Hides the retail and apartments from the yard at ground level | Upper floors need tree height |
| **Evergreens, water feature** | Visual screen, and sound masking near the patio | Little real dB |

A rough rule from appraisal studies is that highway noise costs ~0.5% of home value per dB.
People also tend to find traffic they can't see less annoying.

### Hiding the road (sight lines, solid wall at the line)

The road is 15–20 ft below the lot, so hiding it takes less wall than quieting it. This
assumes the ground is about level from the house to the line and the lanes are 60–100 ft
out. If the yard falls toward the highway, add the drop.

| Viewer | Wall that hides cars, all lanes | Wall that hides trucks, all lanes |
|---|---|---|
| Ground floor, or standing in the yard | ~2–3 ft | ~4–5 ft |
| 2nd floor | up to ~8 ft | up to ~11 ft |

An 8 ft solid wall makes the road disappear from the ground floor and yard and hides most
cars from the 2nd floor. 10–12 ft hides nearly everything. The open end at Candelaria stays
visible unless the wall returns along it.

### Back line (retail and apartments)

Wall height at the back line needed to hide our yard and house (~60–130 ft inside the line)
from apartment windows 20–40 ft past the line. The lot right behind us is the one-story
retail (see "The development behind the back line"), and the apartment lot starts ~77 m
off. So this is the worst case until the apartments' site plan shows how far off and how
tall their buildings are.

| Apartment window | Screen height at the back line |
|---|---|
| 2nd floor (eye ~15 ft) | ~11–14 ft |
| 3rd floor (eye ~25 ft) | ~17–22 ft |
| 4th floor (eye ~35 ft) | ~23–31 ft |

The apartments are 5 stories (owner, Oct 15 2026), so a 5th floor (eye ~45 ft) needs still more:
roughly 30–40 ft at the line. Only tall trees near the back line can screen that, and they
don't need to cover all of it: woods already stand across most of the sight lines (see
"Existing trees from the lidar").

- **A fence only screens ground-level views.** The trees between the back line and the house
  are the real screen for the upper floors. Protect them: the clearing next door may have
  cut their roots, so water in droughts and have an arborist look at them.
- **Screens near where you sit work best.** A tree, pergola or tall evergreens by the patio
  block more of the view per foot of height than a wall 130 ft away.
- **Get both site plans from the City of Austin**, which reviewed them as ETJ cases:
  SP-2019-0411D (retail) and SP-2018-0138D (apartments). They show any required fence,
  buffer or landscaping, building heights and setbacks, parking-lot lighting, and dumpster
  and HVAC locations. We're outside city limits, so Austin's compatibility screening probably
  doesn't protect us. Don't count on the developer's fence until the plan shows it.

#### Back-line trees (Oct 11 2026; low-allergy revision Oct 12 2026)

The site is thin, rocky, alkaline soil over limestone. With the land next door cleared, the
line now takes full west sun. The area has heavy deer and oak wilt.

**No cypress or juniper (Oct 12 2026).** Allergies in the household rule out the whole cypress
family:

Their pollens cross-react. From here on:
- prefer insect-pollinated plants;
- for plants with separate male and female plants, buy the females, which shed no pollen.

### A slab over the buried line? (owner's question, Oct 18 2026)

- **Not over the line as it is, but yes in conduit.**
  - The NEC (300.5 and Table 300.5) lets wiring run under a building only in a raceway
    (conduit), carried out past the outside walls.
  - A line buried as cable, as most older yard runs are, has to be dug up across the
    footprint and replaced or sleeved in conduit. Then there are no splices under the slab.
- **The construction is the bigger risk.** The line is probably 18–24 in down. The pad's
  stripping and compaction would reach it, and so would the thickened edges (12–24 in) and a
  metal building's column piers (often 3–4 ft).
- **Under a slab, a fault can't be dug out,** so the cable can't be repaired in place. In
  conduit it can at least be pulled and replaced.
- **The better fix: reroute it around the pad.**
  - New conduit from the meter pole to the house, in the same trench as building B's own
    feed, plus a spare conduit.
  - It's the owner's line past the meter, so it's the owner's electrician's job.
  - Keep the trench out of the twin oak's root zone and off the drainfield.
- **Which site it hits** (straight routes from the meter pole, `plan3d.py` geometry; the
  real route may bend):

### Tube frame or red-iron: what would justify red-iron (owner's question, Oct 18 2026)

- **Decided the same day: tube frame.** The owner ruled out red-iron; none of the triggers
  below applies. Kept for the record.

- **Both can be certified to the code wind load.** "Sturdier" shows up in five measurable
  places, not in passing code:

  | Measure | Typical tube frame | Typical red-iron |
  |---|---|---|
  | Frame | 14-ga (12-ga option) 2.5 in square tube, a truss every 4–5 ft | I-beam columns and rafters every 20–25 ft, purlins and girts |
  | Hanging loads (hoist, loft, ceiling, roof solar, ducts) | little or none; point loads not designed for | 5–10 psf collateral load typical, plus designed point loads |
  | Panels | 29-ga typical (26-ga is an option) | 26-ga typical: ~25% thicker, dents less in hail |
  | Insulation cavity | ~2.5 in tube depth | ~8 in girts |
  | Span, height, openings | to ~30 ft wide and ~16–20 ft tall; openings limited by the truss spacing | almost any span or height; framed openings anywhere |
  | Expected life | ~20–30 years | 50+ years |

- **Things that would push it to red-iron:**
  - a chain hoist or anything hung from the frame;
  - a loft or mezzanine;
  - a drywall or liner ceiling, or solar panels on the roof;
  - heating and cooling it, with thick insulation;
  - doors bigger than 12 × 12 or close to the corners;
  - eaves over ~16 ft or a span over 30 ft;
  - a horizon long enough for its extra life to count.
- **Resale:** agreed, little or none for a residential outbuilding. Appraisers and buyers see
  "steel building, X sq ft". So the extra life is worth something only if you'd stay.
- **Cost per year, for 30 × 25:**
  - a tube frame building (~$16.5k midpoint) over ~25 years is ~$660 a year;
  - red-iron (~$26k) over ~50 years is ~$520 a year;
  - over a 10–15 year stay, the tube frame is plainly cheaper.
- **Middle ground:** a tube frame upgraded to a 12-ga frame, 26-ga panels, 4 ft truss
  spacing and certified anchoring. It closes much of the gap for a few thousand dollars.
- **Comparing quotes like for like:** ask each for:
  - the certified wind speed and exposure category;
  - the snow and collateral loads (psf);
  - the frame and panel gauges and the truss spacing;
  - whether the roll-up doors are wind-rated (doors are a common failure point on either).

### Does the 15% even apply? Probably not to this lot (Oct 18 2026)

- **15% is the SOS Ordinance's number:** the City's Barton Springs Zone limit for the recharge
  zone, on net site area. It reaches outside the city limits only through the City's
  subdivision and site-plan review in its ETJ.
- **The original 1992 ordinance exempted existing lots** (No. 920903-D, §13-7-36.6): it
  doesn't apply to development of a single-family, single-family attached or duplex
  residential structure on a single platted lot that existed as of November 1, 1991.
- **Our lot existed by then.** It's Lot 176 of Granada Hills Amended (recorded plat, Vol. 55
  Pg. 69). The April 1991 survey already shows the house on it.
- **Travis County** issues the permit for an accessory building outside the city. Its page
  asks for a site plan showing the septic system. The county's own Edwards Aquifer cap found
  so far is 30%, and it's per subdivision, not per lot.
- **Still to confirm:**
  - Is the 1991 existing-lot exemption still in today's code (LDC 25-8), and does it cover an
    accessory building on a single-family lot?
  - Is the lot in Austin's ETJ at all?
  - What did the owner's 15% come from? A deed restriction would be a different matter.
- **Checked against the county code itself** (Chapter 482, amended through Oct 28 2025).
  Another agent had claimed the county caps single-family lots at 15% of gross acreage, citing
  §482.229. The code doesn't say that.
  - **§482.229's 15%** ("Impervious cover shall not exceed fifteen percent (15%) of the
    property's acreage for single family and duplex residential development") is in
    Subchapter C, Conservation Development.
    - That subchapter "applies only outside the ETJ" (§482.220).
    - It binds only an owner who signs a conservation development agreement with the county
      before filing a preliminary plan or final plat (§482.221). It's an elective program for
      new subdivisions.
  - **§482.216** caps new subdivisions over the Edwards Aquifer recharge or contributing zone:
    30% residential, 45% commercial, with exceptions. That's per subdivision.
  - **§482.944(b)(3)** treats "15 percent or less" gross cover as an alternate standard that
    lets a subdivision skip water-quality ponds. It's not a cap.
  - Nothing found in Chapter 482 caps the cover on an existing lot in a recorded subdivision,
    or on its accessory-building permit.
  - The county's guidance asks for permanent water-quality controls only past 10,000 sq ft of
    added cover on a residential lot that isn't in a subdivision. We're neither.
- **Still worth one call or email to Travis County TNR (Development Services):** ask whether any
  impervious-cover limit applies to an accessory building on this lot. Also check the deed
  restrictions, in the title commitment's Schedule B, which the county code wouldn't show.

  - It's under the eave, so put a drip cap over the head, and the gutter keeps the roof's water

- **Why kit doors stick:**
  - The usual tube-frame walk door is a light residential-style steel door. Its knock-down
    frame is screwed to the girts.
  - The girts flex and the frame racks, so the gap at the latch closes.
  - Butt hinges sag the door on its screws.
  - Out of level, the sweep drags.
  - Vinyl weatherstrip goes tacky in the sun.
- **The spec to buy, all standard commercial items:**

  | Part | Spec | Why |
  |---|---|---|
  | Door | Hollow metal, 1-3/4 in, 18 ga (16 if it'll get abuse), galvannealed (A60), insulated core; 3070 (3 × 7 ft), outswing | stiff, flat, doesn't dent or rust |
  | Frame | 16 ga welded (not knock-down), galvannealed; on its own jamb posts and header, anchored to the slab with base clips | it can't rack, so the gap stays even |
  | Hinge | Continuous geared aluminum, full height (e.g. Select SL11 or Pemko CFM) | can't sag; takes the weight along the whole edge |
  | Lever | ANSI/BHMA Grade 1 cylindrical (e.g. Schlage ND) | built for commercial use |
  | Deadbolt | Grade 1, keyed alike with the lever | |
  | Closer | Grade 1, parallel arm on the inside (e.g. LCN 4040XP) | closes and latches it every time |
  | Stop | Overhead stop/holder (e.g. Glynn-Johnson 90) | the wind can't tear an outswing door open |
  | Seals | Adjustable silicone bulb weatherstrip, aluminum threshold, sweep, drip cap | silicone doesn't stick to paint in heat; adjustable keeps the seal from binding |
  | Latch guard | Optional, steel plate over the latch | security |

- **Install so it stays right:**
  - Frame the opening with tube jamb posts from the slab to the eave strut, and a header.
    Don't hang it from girts alone.
  - Set the frame plumb and square with spreaders, and check the slab is level across the
    opening (±1/8 in) before the pour is finished. Ask the slab crew to hold that spot.
  - Look for an even ~1/8 in gap all round the door. Adjust the weatherstrip last, so it
    seals without loading the latch.
  - It can go in after the building has settled for a season, if the dealer's door is left
    out.

### Windows and doors

- Traffic noise is heavy in low frequencies. Compare windows by **OITC** (made for traffic
  noise), not just STC.
- Rough ranges: a standard builder dual-pane is ~OITC 20–24 (STC 26–28). Laminated glass with
  unequal panes and a wider gap is ~OITC 28–31 (STC 32–36). An interior acoustic insert a few
  inches inside the existing window can reach ~STC 40+, often for less than a replacement.
- Seal the frames, use solid-core exterior doors with full seals, and check north-side attic
  and wall vents.
