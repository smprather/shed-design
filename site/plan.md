## Order of work

**The building at site A comes first** (owner, Oct 24 2026): the shed, with a way to drive the
mower in. Its slab is dug in so the doors are at grade. The drive comes much later and is out of every budget for now. The building sits
between the well, the septic pipe and the buried service line, so a few cheap checks come
before its first dig.

### Step 0: before the building's first dig (now)

1. **Find the septic tank and drainfield.** Get the septic permit file from Travis County's
   on-site sewage facility (OSSF) office for this address. It usually has a drawing of the
   tank and drainfield.
   - The slab comes within 13.4 ft of the septic access pipe, and the short block wall by
     it stands on the 10 ft keep-out's edge. Nothing gets dug, poured or driven over the tank.
   - The county's building permit wants a site plan showing the septic system anyway.
   - The US-290 wall footing, the north end of the back-line trees and the septic truck's
     route wait on it too.
   - If there's no drawing, have the septic company locate and probe the tank lids and
     trenches next time it pumps.
2. **Locate the buried electric line.** It runs underground from the meter pole at the NW
   fence corner to the house's NW corner (owner, Oct 18 2026). The route between isn't known.
   - **Look in the pole's disconnect too:** its breaker size and whether it has spare spaces.
     Building A's power comes from there (project 1).
   - **Straight, it would pass over the well,** so it probably bends around it, and maybe
     around the septic tank.
   - **Straight, it would run ~32 ft under site A.**
   - **How to find it:**
     - It's private past the meter, so 811 won't mark it. Hire a private utility locator, or
       rent a cable locator (a transmitter on the line and a hand receiver; tool-rental
       shops have them).
     - A metal detector wouldn't do it: they rarely reach a cable 18–24 in down, and they
       don't follow a line.
     - Clues: which way the conduit sweeps into the ground at the pole and at the house, and
       a settled or off-color stripe in the grass.
   - Have the locator give the depth too.
   - **No slab or cut over the line as it is** (see notes, "A slab over the buried
     line"). Reroute it in new conduit around the building, in the same trench as the
     building's own feeder.
   - Call 811 (Texas811) as well before any digging, for the utility-owned lines.
3. **Stake the US-290 line.** A surveyor sets pins on it. The building sits 5 ft inside the
   surveyed line, and the fence wanders off it. Pin the back line on the same visit, for the
   planting; its fence is partly on the retail lot.
4. **Arborist visit**, one trip:
   - the twin oak: root-zone protection along the crews' route to the building (mats, the
     pump line), and the compacted ground under the parked cars;
   - the big leaning oak propped by the house's west wall;
   - the Ashe junipers at the NW corner: which are male (pollen). That check is best in
     December–January.
5. **Paperwork:**
   - the title commitment's deed restrictions: outbuildings (and their height: the ridge is
     ~17.75 ft), boats and trailers, materials, walls, fences, the
     45 ft building line;
   - Travis County's accessory-building permit, and whether OSSF reviews a new building (or,
     later, a garage conversion) on a septic lot;
   - the kit dealer's drawings stamped for the county's wind load, and the slab's design for
     its anchors.

## Project 1: The building (site A; site B dropped Oct 18 2026)

**At site A, on a concrete slab dug into its high side** (owner, Oct 24 2026): a common
30 × 30 ft tube-frame kit, 30 ft wide across the strip and 30 ft along the line. The ridge

**Built to store a boat** (owner, Oct 25 2026): boat storage adds to a house near Lake Travis,
so the building is ready for one.
- **14 ft legs,** so a 12 ft roll-up fits under the eave with ~2 ft for its coil. The ridge
  is ~17.75 ft up.
- **A 12 × 12 ft door** clears most wake boats with the tower folded, pontoons with the
  bimini down and center consoles without a T-top.
  - A 10 ft door is tight for many of them.
  - Towers up and T-tops often stand over 12 ft. Check a boat's height on its trailer.
- **12 ft wide** leaves room to back in an 8.5 ft boat, the widest towed without a permit.
- **30 ft deep** takes a boat to ~24 ft on its trailer. The trailer adds ~4–5 ft past the
  bow; a swing-away tongue buys ~2 ft.
- The 3D plan has a 22 ft wake boat on its trailer in the west bay, for scale.
- **The boat needs a way in.** A pickup and trailer shouldn't cross the lawn: the way
  passes the twin oak's root zone, and the lawn ruts when it's wet. The shop lane
  (project 4) is the boat's road. Until it's built, the boat bay is a selling point more
  than a working one.

**All the doors face south** (owner, Oct 18 2026), well away from the septic pipe:
- a 12 × 12 ft roll-up at the west end, for a boat;
- a 10 × 12 ft roll-up at the east end. The mower parks in that bay;
- a commercial walk door between them: hollow metal in a welded frame, a continuous
  hinge, Grade 1 hardware and a closer, so it won't sag or stick (notes, "The walk door").
- There's 1 ft of wall between each roll-up and the walk door, and 1.5 ft at each corner.
  The dealer confirms the framing. If it needs more, the walk door can move to the east
  wall.

**Dig in, don't build up.** The ground under it rises ~2.7 ft to the NE corner, but the door

- The NE corner is cut ~1.9 ft (2.6 ft to the bottom of the base).

**Insulation, the high-grade kind** (owner, Oct 24 2026):
- **Closed-cell spray foam:** 2 in (~R-13) on the walls and gable ends, 3 in (~R-20) under
  the roof.
  - It seals the air and stops condensation on the steel, and nothing nests in it.
- **A thermal barrier over the foam:** an intumescent coating.
  - The code wants foam covered (IRC R316.4), and a woodshop has sparks and finishes.
- **The cheaper way:** the dealer's fiberglass blanket (R-13 walls, R-19 roof), ~$11k less.
  It's less airtight, and a tear lets condensation in.
- **The roll-up doors stay uninsulated.** Insulated sectional doors are an option (~$4–7.5k).
